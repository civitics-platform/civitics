## 1. Reads 2–3 — the three facts, the owner, the grants

The design of record has the Vercel route calling `enforce_cron_job_budgets()`
directly over PostgREST. Measured on prod 2026-09-21, that call would have
failed — and it would have failed *silently in the shape that matters*, because
the function's every unsatisfied check is a refusal to cancel, so a permission
failure and "nothing was over budget" are hard to tell apart from the outside.

| fact | measured on prod |
|---|---|
| `enforce_cron_job_budgets` `prosecdef` | **`f`** (SECURITY INVOKER) |
| `enforce_derived_mvs_unit_budget` `prosecdef` | **`f`** |
| owner of both (`proowner::regrole`) | **`postgres`** |
| `proconfig` of both | `{"search_path=public, pg_catalog"}` |
| `has_schema_privilege('service_role','cron','USAGE')` | **`false`** |
| `has_table_privilege('service_role','cron.job_run_details','SELECT')` | **`true`** |

**Grants, before:** `service_role` **does** hold `EXECUTE` on both watchdogs,
exactly as the prompt predicted — Supabase's default grant survived because
every REVOKE in the family (FIX-1063:278, FIX-1101:420, FIX-1030:663,
FIX-1035:224) names `PUBLIC, anon, authenticated` and never `service_role`.

```
 proname                         | grantee      | privilege_type
---------------------------------+--------------+----------------
 enforce_cron_job_budgets        | postgres     | EXECUTE
 enforce_cron_job_budgets        | service_role | EXECUTE

 check_cron_job_health           | postgres     | EXECUTE

```

So EXECUTE was never the missing piece, and granting more of it would not have
helped. Running **as** `service_role`, a SECURITY INVOKER watchdog cannot do its
job in two independent ways: the `FROM cron.job_run_details` read dies at the
**schema** gate (note the *table* SELECT grant is present and unreachable
without schema `USAGE` — a nicely misleading pair), and `pg_cancel_backend`
against a postgres-owned backend would be refused even if the read succeeded.

**Read 3's STOP did not fire.** Both functions are owned by `postgres`. One
refinement to the read's premise, worth recording because FIX-1030's comment is
loose on it: `postgres` on Supabase is **not** superuser, so "a cancel against a
backend of the same role needs no `pg_signal_backend`" is not what is carrying
this. What carries it is that `postgres` **is a member of `pg_signal_backend`**.
The distinction matters for the width argument — a DEFINER function owned by
`postgres` inherits that membership, which is precisely the capability FIX-1030
did not want handed out, and precisely why the wrapper takes no pid.

## 2. Item 1 — the wrapper's width, and the D2 proof

### The primitive, stated in FIX-1030's own terms

`public.run_cron_watchdogs()` is `SECURITY DEFINER SET search_path = public,
cron, pg_catalog`, owned by `postgres`. It is **not** the "cancel any backend"
primitive FIX-1030 refused:

- **No parameters. No pid input.** There is nothing a caller can aim.
- It **CALLs** `enforce_cron_job_budgets()` and
  `enforce_derived_mvs_unit_budget()` — it does not copy their predicates. A
  copy would be a second predicate to keep in step with the first; the entire
  point is one predicate with two firing paths.
- It therefore cancels only what those two already select: for the budget arm, a
  `running` `cron.job_run_details` row whose jobname is in `cron_job_budget`,
  past `budget_seconds`, with a non-null `job_pid`, no prior ledger row for that
  `runid`, and a live non-idle `pg_stat_activity` row whose query matches the
  CALL target parsed out of the job's own command; for the unit arm, the single
  `running` `refresh_derived_mvs` row's published `metadata.backend_pid`, past
  its unit budget, not already stamped `watchdog_canceled_at`.
- Whoever holds EXECUTE gains exactly one capability: *"run the two watchdogs
  now."* They cannot name a target, widen a predicate, or raise a budget.

Each inner call sits in its own `BEGIN … EXCEPTION WHEN OTHERS` so one failing
surfaces in the payload rather than blinding its sibling — the shape FIX-1101
already uses for its own `ec_window` call.

**No `statement_timeout` anywhere**, neither as proconfig nor as a body `SET`
(FIX-1128: inert either way). The header names the two real bounds so nobody
adds a third: `authenticator`'s 8 s role GUC inherited by `service_role`, and
the route's 10 s client race. `check:proconfig` — 15 migrations scanned, 0
waived, **0 undeclared**.

`acted_via` is `text NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pg_cron'` with a CHECK for the closed
two-value vocabulary. The inner INSERT stamps the default, so history reads
`pg_cron` and every pg_cron firing continues to; the wrapper re-labels only the
runids **its own call** returned, guarded by `acted_via = 'pg_cron'` (never
steals a row an overlapping pg_cron firing claimed) and `acted_at >= now() -
interval '5 minutes'` (a recycled runid cannot reach back into history).

### The D2 proof — the real PostgREST path, on the clone's live scheduler

Local pg_cron was live (21 active jobs, 10 runs in the preceding 10 minutes) and
local PostgREST answered 200, so the **full** end-to-end proof ran; no fallback
to `SET ROLE` was needed.

One deviation from D2 as written, and it makes the proof stronger rather than
weaker: **the local `cron-job-budget-watchdog` was parked for the duration**
(`cron.alter_job(active := false)`, restored in teardown). Both `*/2` jobs are
active on the clone, so leaving them running would have let pg_cron's own
watchdog race the call and stamp `pg_cron`, making the result a coin toss. With
it parked, the only path that could cancel anything was the one under test —
which is also the exact condition P2-A exists for.

## 3. Item 2 — the route, the verdict module, the Vercel entry

**Route:** `apps/civitics/app/api/cron/cron-watchdog/route.ts` —
`force-dynamic`, `maxDuration = 30`, the CRON_SECRET check verbatim from
`front-door-watch:186-190`, the `CRON_DISABLED` short-circuit from
`nightly-sync:47-50`, the lazy `createAdminClient` inside the try (FIX-1130's
reason: a module-scope client makes the route fail before it can report
anything), and
`withDbTimeout(db.rpc("run_cron_watchdogs"), 10_000, "cron-watchdog")`.

It writes **no `data_sync_log` row**, deliberately: at `*/2` that would be 720
rows/day of breadcrumb, and the FIX-1011 freshness registry would then try to
infer a cadence for it. The durable trace already exists and is strictly better
because it is written only when something happened — the ledger's new
`acted_via`, and the unit watchdog's own stamp on the `refresh_derived_mvs` row.

It answers **200 on failure** (401 on auth, as siblings do). Vercel retries
nothing on a cron 5xx, so a 5xx buys no recovery and only reddens a dashboard
until the reader learns to ignore it; the failure goes in the body and in a
`console.error` line, which is where it can be found.

**Verdict module:** `packages/db/src/cron-watchdog-verdict.ts` +
`.test.ts`, exported from `index.ts` — the `front-door-verdict.ts` pattern. It
parses defensively because the jsonb it reads is assembled by three different
migrations (FIX-1063's loop, FIX-1101's `ec_window` arm, FIX-1030/1035's unit
watchdog) and nothing type-checks that boundary.

The design decision worth naming: **`ok` is about the call, not about whether
anything was cancelled.** `canceled: 0` is the correct answer for roughly 719 of
every 720 daily calls; treating it as a failure would turn the route into a
720/day siren nobody reads. `ok` is false only when an inner watchdog actually
threw or the payload did not parse.

Seven tests. Three fixtures are real shapes — two of them the D2 proof's own
payloads verbatim — and the load-bearing one is `innerErrorFixture`: the budget
arm throws, and the test asserts the unit arm's genuine `canceled` **survives**,
with `checked: null` rather than a false zero. A verdict that collapsed "one arm
threw" into a bare `ok: false` would be useless in exactly the incident this
route exists for.

**Vercel entry:** `apps/civitics/vercel.json` gains
`{"path": "/api/cron/cron-watchdog", "schedule": "*/2 * * * *"}` — the fifth
cron.

**Deploy check: NOT MADE from this shell.** `vercel ls` needs device OAuth

## 4. Item 3 — receipts §9 "The forker"

New section `## 9. The forker (FIX-1194) — job startup timeouts and who was
running`; "Not capturable here" becomes `## 10.`; the heading-order test and its
name ("all nine sections" → "all ten") follow.

Four queries, all registered through `Reader.run` and listed in `queryBlock`,
all on FIX-1073's predicate (`status='failed' AND return_message ILIKE
'%startup timeout%'`):

| key | what |
|---|---|
| `forker_24h` | by UTC hour, last 24 h, non-zero hours only |
| `forker_7d` | by day, last 7 days — the cc-139 §1d episodic-vs-chronic shape |
| `forker_running` | per burst hour, the longest **overlapping** job that was not itself a startup timeout |
| `forker_cancels` | budget cancels, last 7 days, with `acted_via` |

**One deviation from the design's SQL, and it is load-bearing:** the LATERAL
uses `LEFT JOIN cron.job`, not the inner join the prompt sketched. pg_cron
**deletes** the `cron.job` row on unschedule while `job_run_details` survives,
so an inner join would silently drop exactly the historical runs this section
exists for. An orphaned run renders `(unscheduled jobid N)`.

### The threshold test carries the weight

`BURST_THRESHOLD = 3`. One startup timeout in an hour is ordinary scheduler
noise — FIX-1073's tiers exist because the rate is never zero — and pairing each
one with "the longest job running at the time" would manufacture a suspect for
every hour of every day. The third table is only worth reading because it is
sparse.

So the fixture carries a deliberate near-miss: a **2-failure** hour
(`2026-09-07 04:00`) alongside the real 22-failure burst (`2026-09-07 06:00`,
cc-139's 12/30 + 10/30). The test slices the rendered markdown between
`### Who was running` and `### Budget cancels` and asserts the 22-failure hour
**is** named and the 2-failure hour **is not**.

The section also says in prose that an overlap is a **suspect, not a verdict**,
and that a burst hour naming *nothing* is the more interesting outcome — which
is what cc-139 actually found for 09-07.

### Rendered against the clone

`pnpm --filter @civitics/data data:receipts:daily` (LOCAL, nominal day
2026-09-22). All four tables render `_(no rows)_`; queries cost 17 / 16 / 17 ms.

That is truthful, not a dead predicate, and it was checked rather than assumed.
The clone holds **16,490** `job_run_details` rows back to 2026-06-30 and

## 8. What contradicted the design, the bullets, or this prompt

### (a) The design's P2-A could not have worked as written — and the prompt already knew

This is the one the prompt told me to lead with, and it holds exactly as
predicted. Design §3 P2-A has the route calling `enforce_cron_job_budgets()`
over PostgREST. `service_role` holds EXECUTE on it, so the call would have been
*permitted* — and then failed inside, at the `cron` schema gate, because
`has_schema_privilege('service_role','cron','USAGE')` is `false`.

What makes it worth more than a footnote is the **failure shape**. Every
unsatisfied check in that function is a deliberate refusal to cancel, and it
returns a well-formed jsonb either way. Had the wrapper not existed and the
route called the function directly, the most likely outcome was not a loud
error — it was a permission failure inside a function whose entire design
philosophy is to fail quiet, reported to a route that logs one line. A guard
that reports "checked 0, canceled 0" because it cannot see is indistinguishable
from one reporting "nothing was over budget", and that is the FIX-1130 failure
wearing a third costume.

The prompt's read 2 is what caught it, and its rule 148 framing — *check a
mechanism against process boundaries before building on it* — is the reusable
part.

### (b) FIX-1030's own comment is slightly wrong about why it works

FIX-1030 says: *"pg_cancel_backend against a postgres-owned backend needs
superuser or pg_signal_backend; the pg_cron job runs as postgres and therefore
has it."* Read 3 in the prompt restates this as "a cancel against a backend of
the same role needs no `pg_signal_backend`".

Measured: `postgres` on Supabase is **not** superuser (`rolsuper = f`). It is a
**member of `pg_signal_backend`** (`pg_has_role(...) = t`). So the disjunction's
second arm is what is load-bearing, not the first, and not same-role identity.

That matters for the width argument rather than for the code. A DEFINER
function owned by `postgres` inherits *role membership*, which is broader than
"can signal its own backends" — it is exactly the "cancel any backend"
capability FIX-1030 declined to hand out. Which is why the narrowness of the
wrapper (`pronargs = 0`, no pid, CALLs rather than copies) is not stylistic: it
is the entire safety argument, and the catalog now records it.

### (c) An inner join in §9's SQL would have dropped the rows the section exists for

D4 specifies the burst-hour query as
`FROM cron.job_run_details d JOIN cron.job j USING (jobid)`. pg_cron **deletes**
the `cron.job` row on `unschedule` while `job_run_details` survives, so an inner
join silently drops every run whose job has since been unscheduled — which is
disproportionately the historical, incident-adjacent runs a forensic section is
for. Changed to `LEFT JOIN` with `COALESCE(j.jobname, '(unscheduled jobid ' ||
d.jobid || ')')`. Same finding class as FIX-1190: a join that looks like
plumbing deciding what the reader is allowed to see.

### (d) `docs/OPERATIONS.md` recorded a Vercel plan the repo outgrew in April

D3 asked me to correct "its stale Hobby / daily crons line". There were
**three** stale sites, not one, and a fourth error next door:

1. The environment table: *"Vercel plan — Hobby, crons limited to once/day"*.
2. The monthly-cost table: *"Vercel | Hobby | $0 | crons limited to once/day"*.
3. A **Vercel Hobby** plan-limits block asserting *"Cron jobs: once per day
   maximum (upgrade to Pro for sub-daily crons — currently `notify-followers` is
   at 03:00 UTC)"*.

All three are wrong and were already falsified by the tree:
`platform-snapshot/route.ts:10-11` says Pro since the April cutover, and four
sub-daily entries (`*/30`, `*/15`) have been live in `vercel.json` since
FIX-1127 / FIX-1130 — Hobby rejects those expressions outright, so their mere
existence is the proof.

The fourth: the `CRON_DISABLED` kill-switch row claims it *"stops the two Vercel
cron routes"* and names `nightly-sync` and `notify-followers`. Grepped: the flag
is read by `nightly-sync`, `notify-followers` and now `cron-watchdog` — **three**
— and is **not** read by `platform-snapshot` or `front-door-watch`. That is a
kill switch documented as covering more than it does, which is the dangerous
direction for a kill switch. Corrected, with the gap named explicitly and a note
that it now silences the Vercel half of budget enforcement while leaving
pg_cron's half running.

**One thing I did not do:** I left the Vercel **cost** cell as `see note` marked
unverified rather than substituting a figure. The plan name is provable from the
tree; the price is not, and inventing "$20/mo" to make a table look finished is
how a cost table becomes fiction. It is flagged in place with a pointer to the
billing API.

### (e) The clone rendered §9 empty, and that needed proving rather than asserting

Every table came back `_(no rows)_` on the clone. That is the shape a broken
predicate also produces, so it was checked: the clone holds 16,490
`job_run_details` rows back to 2026-06-30 and **exactly one** all-time startup
timeout, on 2026-09-05 — outside both windows. Re-running the identical
predicate at 60 days returns it. The section is empty because the clone is
quiet, not because the query is dead.

Worth recording as a caveat on the clone as an instrument for this section:
local pg_cron history is *local* history (CLAUDE.md's FIX-946 note, generalised
from `cron.job` to `job_run_details`). The clone can never evidence prod's fork
behaviour, so §9's real first reading is the next prod receipts run.

### (f) A generated prod artifact was nearly committed with clone data in it

Running `data:receipts:daily` against LOCAL overwrote the committed

## 9. Correction — the cron was firing all along (FIX-1207 closed no-op, FIX-1208 filed)

Written after this report's first commit, and deliberately appended rather than
folded back into §5 and §8(i). Those sections are left exactly as they were
written.

### What is true

The `*/2` Vercel cron fires on schedule. Craig's function log:

```
Sep 21 19:14:18.31  GET 200  /api/cron/cron-watchdog
  [cron/cron-watchdog] budget checked=0 canceled=0 unit=none via=vercel
Sep 21 19:12:18.18  GET 200  ...   (same line)
Sep 21 19:10:18.14  GET 200  ...   (same line)
Sep 21 19:08:18.12  GET 200  ...   (same line)
```

Those are **PDT**: 19:08–19:14 PDT is **02:08–02:14 UTC** — the same minutes in
which I read `pg_stat_statements`, saw `2`, and reported "not firing". The cron
was firing *through* my measurement window.

It is also a stronger result than a bare 200: the line is my own verdict
module's output, and `via=vercel` plus `checked=0` are read out of the RPC's
returned jsonb — a null payload renders `UNPARSEABLE`, an RPC error logs
`RPC failed`. So `run_cron_watchdogs()` executed and returned a well-formed
result on each firing. **P2-A is deployed and running.**

### What I got wrong, and why the method is the transferable part

I used a `pg_stat_statements` counter as a proxy for *"did the route run"*. I
validated that proxy against **one direct curl**, saw it increment, and then
trusted it for a **different path** — a call from the deployed route — which I
never validated it against.

A proxy confirmed on path A and then trusted on path B is not an instrument, it
is an assumption. This one failed in the worst direction: it manufactured a 🔴
finding out of a healthy system, and everything *around* the false claim was
solid enough to make it look earned — CI green, `cc:verify` passing, catalog
reads all correct, a control read (`front-door-watch` at 01:00/01:15/01:30) and
a stated confidence interval.

That control is the sharpest part. It correctly told me Vercel cron worked in
general. I read it as *narrowing* the fault to my cron; the same reading should
have raised my suspicion of **the counter**, because "Vercel works, this one
route doesn't, and the only evidence is one metric" is at least as much a story
about the metric. I chose the hypothesis that made my measurement right.

I also over-corrected once in the other direction mid-investigation: I told
Craig the 1 → 2 rise "is the Vercel scheduler firing" on a single data point,
then had to walk it back fifteen minutes later. Both errors have the same root
— treating one reading of an unvalidated proxy as a fact.
